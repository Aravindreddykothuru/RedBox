## 11.3 RQ1: Do deep models beat well-tuned classic collaborative filtering?

Paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests on per-user NDCG@10 (6,034 users, Holm-corrected; all p < 10⁻⁵):

| Comparison | Relative difference | Users better / worse / tied |
|---|---:|---|
| EASE vs LightGCN | +10.1% | 1,412 / 1,179 / 3,443 |
| EASE vs BPR-MF | +9.4% | 1,375 / 1,167 / 3,492 |
| EASE vs iALS | +11.5% | 1,328 / 1,103 / 3,603 |
| EASE vs NeuMF | +16.1% | 1,451 / 1,135 / 3,448 |
| EASE vs Mult-VAE | +16.3% | 1,476 / 1,114 / 3,444 |
| SASRec (CE) vs EASE | +102.8% | 2,877 / 1,215 / 1,942 |

**Answer.** *No non-sequential deep model beats a well-tuned linear model.*
- EASE, a closed-form linear autoencoder trained in about 2 seconds, is significantly more accurate than the
  graph neural network (LightGCN, 40 minutes of training), the neural MF model (NeuMF) and the variational
  autoencoder (Mult-VAE).
- This independently confirms the reproducibility studies of Ferrari Dacrema et al. (2019) and Rendle et al.
  (2020): progress claimed for deep models often disappears against properly tuned baselines.

The exception is the **sequential Transformer (SASRec)**. Its large advantage is partly real and partly an
artifact of how MovieLens collected ratings (Section 11.4).

**SASRec's loss function matters.**
- **The two variants.** The original paper trains SASRec with binary cross-entropy (BCE) against one sampled
  negative per position; that version scores NDCG@10 = 0.1382. Training the same network with full-softmax
  cross-entropy (CE) over the whole catalogue scores 0.1643 (+18.8%), with higher coverage (0.640 vs 0.554).
- **Agreement with the literature.** This matches the finding that much of the reported gap between SASRec
  and newer Transformers disappears once SASRec is trained with cross-entropy (Klenitskiy & Vasilev, 2023).
- **What we use.** This project uses the CE variant everywhere else.

**Ties.** Many users are "tied" in each comparison. That is almost always because *neither* model found any
of the user's next liked movies: even EASE misses completely for 64% of users. Predicting someone's next
likes is hard.

## 11.4 Why sequential models win on MovieLens: the rating-session artifact

On MovieLens-1M, the trivial Markov chain ("what do people rate right after the movie you rated last?")
beats EASE by 35%. It has no parameters and is "trained" in 0.3 seconds. That does not look like a property of taste, so we
grouped users by the time between their last known rating and their first test rating:

| First test rating came… | Users | EASE | Markov | SASRec (CE) | Hybrid |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| in the same session (< 1 hour) | 5,774 (96%) | 0.081 | 0.112 | 0.168 | **0.183** |
| the same day (1–24 h) | 41 | **0.121** | 0.080 | 0.091 | 0.108 |
| more than a day later | 219 | 0.085 | 0.062 | 0.067 | **0.087** |

**Interpretation**
- **The rating behaviour.** 96% of MovieLens test users continue a rating session in which they rated
  movies in bulk on pages arranged by the website (Harper & Konstan, 2015).
- **What the sequential models exploit.** Within such a session, the next movie is strongly predicted by the
  *display order*, which the sequential models learn (Fan et al., 2023).
- **Taste models win when users come back later.** For users who return on a later day, the order signal is
  gone and EASE beats both sequential models.
- **The hybrid is best in the two large groups** (same session, and more than a day later). It is second,
  behind EASE, in the small same-day group (41 users).

**Order ablation.** We retrained SASRec on randomly shuffled histories, with every other setting identical
(config `ml-1m-ablations.yaml`). The test set is untouched, so this directly measures how much of SASRec's
score comes from the order of ratings:

| SASRec (CE loss) trained on… | NDCG@10 | Recall@10 | HR@10 | Coverage@10 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| histories in time order | **0.1643** | **0.1796** | **0.5621** | **0.640** |
| the same histories, shuffled | 0.0633 (−61%) | 0.0717 | 0.3000 | 0.397 |
| *for reference: EASE (order-blind)* | *0.0810* | *0.0935* | *0.3629* | *0.232* |

Without order information the Transformer falls **below EASE**, to the level of NeuMF and Mult-VAE. In other
words, all of SASRec's advantage over the taste models comes from the sequence, and on MovieLens most of
that sequence is the rating session.

**Consequence for the report.** High accuracy numbers for sequential models on MovieLens must be read with
care. A recommender is only as good as the way its offline data was collected.

## 11.5 RQ2: The proposed Red Box hybrid

| | MovieLens-1M | Amazon |
|---|---:|---:|
| Best single model (SASRec, CE) | 0.1643 | 0.0849 |
| **Red Box hybrid** | **0.1790 (+9.0%)** | **0.0919 (+8.3%)** |
| Significance (Wilcoxon, hybrid vs SASRec) | p = 8.0 × 10⁻²⁷ | p = 2.3 × 10⁻²⁷ |
| Candidate recall (share of test targets among the candidates) | 0.438 | 0.438 (validation) |

**What the ranker learned.** LightGBM LambdaRank relies most on SASRec's rank (52% of its total gain). The
next most important signals are:
- EASE's taste score (7%);
- SASRec's score;
- the user's history length;
- release year;
- LightGCN;
- Markov;
- similarity to the last movie and genre match.

It learns *when* to trust the sequential signal. For users returning after a day it ranks close to EASE
(Section 11.4), where SASRec alone falls behind.

**Diversity (MMR).** Lowering λ trades accuracy for variety smoothly. The app exposes this as a slider.

| λ | NDCG@10 | Intra-list diversity@10 | Coverage@10 |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1.0 (off) | 0.1790 | 0.635 | 0.532 |
| 0.9 | 0.1768 | 0.686 | 0.522 |
| 0.8 | 0.1716 (−4%) | 0.738 (+16%) | 0.511 |
| 0.7 | 0.1653 | 0.784 | 0.500 |
| 0.5 | 0.1467 (−18%) | 0.858 (+35%) | 0.480 |

**The deployed variant.** The app needs models that can score a brand-new user instantly, so it uses only
EASE, SASRec, Markov, iALS (fold-in) and MostPop, without LightGCN. Evaluated once on the same test set, it
scores **0.1789**, the same as the full hybrid (0.1790).

## 11.6 RQ3: Accuracy versus beyond-accuracy, and who is served well

- **Popularity bias.** MostPop recommends only 3% of the catalog. EASE covers 23%. BPR-MF, NeuMF, Mult-VAE
  and LightGCN cover 41–47%. SASRec covers 64% with the highest novelty.
- **Coverage and accuracy are not opposites.** The two most accurate systems also cover the catalog widely:
  SASRec 64%, hybrid 53%.
- **Within-list diversity is a separate dimension.** MostPop's single list mixes many genres (high ILD) while
  covering almost nothing across users. The report therefore shows both measures.
- **User activity.** EASE leads the non-sequential models most clearly for users with short histories. The
  hybrid is the best system in every activity group (figure `results_ndcg_by_activity.png`).

## 11.11 Threats to validity

1. **One chronological split per dataset** (seed 42). Tie-breaking randomness affects 58% of MovieLens users'
   split boundaries. Repeating with other split seeds would give confidence intervals. This is planned as
   future work, because it is CPU-bound.
2. **Unequal tuning in practice.** SASRec and LightGCN could not be tuned within the budget and use their
   papers' settings. This can only *understate* them, so the conclusion that EASE beats LightGCN is
   conservative in LightGCN's favour.
3. **MovieLens session artifact.** It inflates sequential models (Section 11.4). Amazon was added
   specifically to check every conclusion on data without it.
4. **Amazon filtering.** We used a k-core filter (≥ 5 liked items per user, ≥ 15 per item) so EASE fits in
   memory, and ran a subset of models (no NeuMF or Mult-VAE). Fast models were tuned for Amazon; the deep
   models kept their MovieLens settings.
5. **"Liked" = rating ≥ 4.** Implicit-feedback results depend on this threshold. An ablation with every
   rating counting as positive is future work.

## 11.12 The app's models on MovieLens-32M

The web app does not serve the research models above. It serves the same five generators (EASE, SASRec,
iALS, Markov chain, MostPop) and the same LightGBM ranker, retrained on recent data.

- **Data:** MovieLens-32M ratings made from January 2018 to October 2023.
  - Filtering: users with at least 5 liked films and films with at least 20 likes.
  - Result: 42,144 users and 8,487 films.
  - Train/validation/test ratings: 2,820,227 / 355,408 / 355,408.
  - The protocol is unchanged: a per-user chronological 80/10/10 split, ranking over the full catalogue, and
    one use of the test set.
- **Settings:** the tuned MovieLens-1M values, except that SASRec reads only the last 50 films, so a laptop
  CPU can train it. SASRec stopped early at epoch 34. Fitting and refitting it took 3.6 hours.
- **Samples:** the feature table for all users does not fit in laptop memory. So the ranker is trained on
  15,000 validation users, and the served hybrid is evaluated on 15,000 test users, both drawn with a
  fixed seed.

Test NDCG@10 on the same 15,000 test users:

| Model | NDCG@10 |
|---|---:|
| **Red Box hybrid (served)** | **0.1293 (+16.0%)** |
| SASRec (CE loss) | 0.1115 |
| EASE | 0.0913 |
| iALS | 0.0810 |
| Markov chain | 0.0735 |
| MostPop | 0.0377 |

- **The single models rank in the same order as on MovieLens-1M:** SASRec first, EASE the best
  non-sequential model.
- **The hybrid improves on SASRec by 16.0%** (+9.0% on MovieLens-1M). 48.4% of test users find at least
  one film they went on to like in its top ten.
- **Absolute scores are lower than on MovieLens-1M** (0.1293 against 0.1790). The catalogue is 2.3 times
  larger, so ranking over it is harder, and scores should not be compared across datasets.
- **The gain is highly significant.**
  - `recsys serving-eval -c ml-32m` repeats the export's test evaluation with the same seed, so on the same
    15,000 users. It uses the trained models and ranker unchanged and keeps every user's result.
  - On per-user NDCG@10, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test with Holm correction gives:

    | Red Box hybrid vs | Relative difference | p-value (Holm) |
    |---|---:|---:|
    | SASRec (CE loss) | +15.9% | 1.3 × 10⁻⁸⁷ |
    | EASE | +41.6% | 2.2 × 10⁻¹⁶³ |
    | iALS | +59.6% | 1.3 × 10⁻²⁴¹ |
    | Markov chain | +76.0% | < 10⁻³⁰⁰ |
    | MostPop | +243% | < 10⁻³⁰⁰ |

  - Over all 42,144 test users, SASRec scores 0.1125; on the sample, 0.1115.
- **Sources:**
  - `docs/results/ml-32m/serving/`: `test.csv`, `significance.csv` and `README.md`;
  - single models over all users: `docs/results/ml-32m/default_hparams/`.

## 11.13 Trending and brand-new users: an ML forecast instead of popularity

Two places had no personal history to work with, the trending row and a user who has not rated anything yet,
and used plain popularity. Both now use a crowd forecast (Section 8.5).

**How it was checked** (MovieLens-32M; reproduce with `recsys forecast -c ml-32m`; results in
`docs/results/ml-32m/forecast/forecast.csv`):
- The hybrid's top 10 was summed over 4,000 dataset viewers, chosen with seed 42.
- It was judged against what viewers liked next: the test split, which no model saw.
- **Trending:** each method ranks all films; this ranking is compared with the films most viewers liked
  next, with NDCG using the number of such viewers as the gain.
- **Cold start:** 5,000 *other* viewers were treated as brand-new users. Each "chose" their three most-liked
  genres, and their 10 recommendations were compared with what they liked next.

| Use | Method | NDCG@10 | Other measure |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| Trending | Popularity | 0.537 | top-20 overlap 15% |
| Trending | **ML crowd forecast** | **0.961** | **top-20 overlap 70%** |
| New user | Popularity | 0.0184 | hit@10 10.3% |
| New user | Popular in the chosen genres | 0.0182 | hit@10 10.4% |
| New user | ML crowd forecast (no genres) | 0.0297 | hit@10 16.9% |
| New user | **ML: viewers like you (chosen genres)** | **0.0303** | **hit@10 17.2%** |

- **Popularity mostly lists films that most viewers have already seen.** It cannot say what comes *next*.
  The forecast can, and its trending top 10 almost matches the films viewers really liked next.
- **For a new user, the ML forecast finds a liked film in the top ten 67% more often than popularity.**
  Choosing genres adds a little on top of the overall forecast.
- **A plain genre filter on popularity does not help.** The gain comes from the models' predictions, not
  from the genre choice alone.

## 11.14 New films nobody has rated: item cold start

The method is described in Section 8.5: a ridge regression maps each film's MiniLM text vector to its 64
iALS factors.

**How it was checked** (MovieLens-32M; reproduce with `recsys newfilms -c ml-32m`; results in
`docs/results/ml-32m/newfilms/cold_start.csv`):
- A random 10% of the catalogue (848 films) is **hidden as if new**: no ratings and no factors.
- The mapping is learned on the other films. The ridge strength (α = 1) is chosen on held-out training
  films, where R² = 0.18.
- For 5,000 test viewers who went on to like a hidden film, only the hidden films are ranked. Their
  histories exclude the hidden films.
- The ranking is compared with what each viewer liked next (the test split).

| Method | hit@10 | NDCG@10 |
|---|---:|---:|
| Random order | 2.1% | 0.0073 |
| Content only: cosine of the film's text to the viewer's liked films | 5.0% | 0.0192 |
| **Text mapped to iALS factors (ours)** | **18.9%** | **0.0739** |
| Upper bound: the hidden films' true iALS factors | 31.2% | 0.1209 |

- **Mapping into the collaborative space is 3.8 times better than comparing texts directly.** The mapping
  learns which descriptions go with which audiences, not only which words look alike.
- **It recovers about 61% of the upper bound's NDCG** even though R² is only 0.18. Ranking needs the
  direction of the factors, not their exact values.
- **Limitation:** iALS was trained with the hidden films present, so the other films' factors carry a
  trace of them. Retraining iALS without them would give a slightly stricter test.
- **In the app:** 3,146 films at build time (980 English and 183 Hindi from 2023; 464 Telugu, 741 Tamil,
  290 Kannada and 488 Malayalam from 2016) come from Wikidata and Wikipedia, with their posters (96%) and
  summaries. None is in MovieLens, and each is ranked for each user by this mapping.

## 11.15 New films learning from their first ratings (warm-up)

The method is described in Section 8.5: each rating re-estimates the film's factors, starting from the
story's guess (collaborative topic regression).

**How it was checked** (MovieLens-32M; reproduce with `recsys newfilms -c ml-32m`; results in
`docs/results/ml-32m/newfilms/warmup.csv`):
- The same 848 films as in Section 11.14 are treated as new.
- The dataset's viewers are split at random into two halves:
  - **raters:** for each hidden film, the first n of its likers in this half "rate" it, and its factors
    are re-estimated from the story's guess and those n likes;
  - **viewers:** those who went on to like a hidden film get only the hidden films ranked, as in 11.14.
- The prior strength (lambda = 30) was chosen on the validation targets, then tested once.

| Likes per film | hit@10 | NDCG@10 |
|---|---:|---:|
| 0 (the story's guess only) | 20.3% | 0.0797 |
| 1 | 20.1% | 0.0797 |
| 3 | 21.1% | 0.0826 |
| 10 | 22.0% | 0.0874 |
| 30 (23 on average: some films have fewer likers) | 25.7% | 0.0999 |
| Upper bound: the films' true iALS factors | 33.2% | 0.1254 |

- **Every rating helps, and the gain grows steadily with the number of likes:** with about 30 likes, a
  new film closes 44% of the gap between the story's guess and a fully trained film.
- **One like changes little, by design.** The prior keeps a single rating from overturning what the
  story says; the validation data preferred a fairly strong prior (lambda = 30 out of 1 to 100).
- The 0-likes row differs slightly from Section 11.14 because only half of the viewers are evaluated
  here (the other half are raters).
